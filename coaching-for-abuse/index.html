Emotional Abuse

♥ Emotional Abuse slaughters the heart. ♥

No more saying "yes" when my heart is saying "no".

Heart to Heart Life Coaching recognizes that violence is rising at an alarming rate.
1 in every 3 women have experienced abuse in their life time.
1 in every 10 men have experienced abuse in their life time.
It is stated that domestic violence is the leading cause of death and injury in the United States.

There is no race, age, sexual orientation, gender, religious affiliation, educational background or socioeconomic level that domestic abuse and domestic violence does not affect.

Emotional abuse is not always easy to recognize. The abusive person may appear kind, charming, and caring to others while behaving very differently in private. Behind closed doors, their partner or family members may experience intimidation, humiliation, manipulation, threats, or violence. This difference between public behavior and private treatment can leave those experiencing abuse feeling confused, isolated, and unsure whether others will believe them.

Recognizing emotional abuse can be especially painful when you remember the affection and connection you once shared—or still experience between hurtful episodes. You may find yourself hoping that the caring behavior will return. Emotional attachment, financial concerns, children, faith, fear, and safety concerns can make decisions about the relationship complicated. Difficulty leaving does not mean you accept the abuse, and the abuse is never your fault.

Abuse can take many forms, including emotional, psychological, physical, sexual, financial, and spiritual abuse. Neglect and controlling behavior can also cause lasting harm. Emotional abuse deserves to be taken seriously, even when there are no visible injuries. Repeated criticism, humiliation, manipulation, isolation, and efforts to undermine your sense of reality can erode your confidence, self-trust, and sense of safety.

Some abusive behaviors may overlap with traits commonly described as narcissistic, such as entitlement, exploitation, and a lack of empathy. However, abusive behavior alone does not establish a diagnosis. You do not need a label or diagnosis to recognize harmful treatment or seek support.

YOUR ABUSIVE PARTNER DOESN’T THINK THEY HAVE A PROBLEM WITH THEIR ANGER;
THEY HAVE A PROBLEM WITH YOUR ANGER FOR THE TERRIBLE WAY THEY TREAT YOU!

Emotionally abusive behavior can leave you feeling as though you are not allowed to be angry about the way you are being treated. The person hurting you may expect you to remain calm and quiet while treating their own anger as justified. When you express hurt or frustration, they may shift attention away from their behavior and focus entirely on your reaction.

Sherée calls this “STARTING IN THE MIDDLE.” Instead of acknowledging what led to your anger, the story begins with your response—as though it happened without a cause. Your reaction may then be used to portray you as unreasonable or overly emotional, while the treatment that prompted it goes unaddressed.

Over time, feeling pressured to silence your emotions can leave you feeling trapped, unheard, and disconnected from yourself. You may begin questioning whether your feelings are valid or whether you have the right to speak up. Your anger deserves to be understood. It can signal that you have been hurt, that a boundary has been crossed, or that something needs attention. You deserve space to acknowledge those feelings and choose how to respond in ways that support your safety and well-being.

“You’re too sensitive” can become another way of dismissing your experience. When you hear it repeatedly, you may start doubting your perceptions instead of recognizing the impact of someone else’s behavior.

Sensitivity is not a flaw. It can be a capacity for empathy, awareness, and meaningful connection. You do not have to become less sensitive to deserve respectful treatment. Your feelings deserve consideration, and your concerns deserve to be heard.
